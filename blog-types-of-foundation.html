Types of Foundation in Building Construction

Shallow and deep foundation types explained: isolated, combined, strip and raft footings, piles and caissons, how engineers choose, a sizing example and site mistakes to avoid.

Brikito Editorial24 Nov 20266 min readConstruction basics

The short answer: Building foundations are either shallow or deep. Shallow foundations include isolated (pad) footings, combined footings, strip footings and raft foundations. They suit firm soil near the surface. Deep foundations carry loads down to stronger layers below and include pile foundations (end-bearing or friction, bored or driven) and caissons. The right type depends on the building loads, the soil's bearing capacity, the water table and neighbouring structures, and is chosen by a structural engineer after a soil investigation.

The foundation is the one part of a building nobody sees and everybody depends on. It carries the weight of the structure into the ground without letting it sink unevenly. Choose the wrong type, or build it carelessly, and the cracks show up years later, when they are hardest and most expensive to fix.

This guide explains the main types of foundation in building construction, when each is used, how one is sized in a simple example, and the site mistakes to avoid.

What is a foundation?

A foundation is the lowest part of a structure. It transfers the loads from columns and walls to the soil, spreading them so the soil isn't overstressed and the building settles evenly. Foundations are grouped by depth: shallow foundations sit close to the surface, while deep foundations transfer load to stronger soil or rock further down.

Types of shallow foundation

1. Isolated footing (pad footing)

A single square, rectangular or circular pad under each column. It is the most common foundation for low- and mid-rise RCC framed buildings on firm soil.

2. Combined footing

One footing supporting two or more columns. Used when columns are close together, or when a column near a boundary can't have its own footing centred under it.

3. Strip footing (wall footing)

A continuous strip under a load-bearing wall or a line of closely spaced columns. Common in load-bearing masonry buildings.

4. Raft foundation (mat foundation)

A single thick slab under the whole building or a large part of it. Used when soil bearing capacity is low, loads are heavy, or individual footings would cover more than about half the plan area. It also helps with basements and uneven settlement.

Types of deep foundation

5. Pile foundation

Long columns of concrete or steel that carry load deep into the ground. End-bearing piles rest on a hard layer or rock. Friction piles carry load through friction along their length. Piles can be bored cast-in-situ, which is common in Indian cities because it causes less vibration, or driven (precast). A pile cap ties a group of piles to each column.

6. Pier and caisson foundations

Large-diameter shafts sunk to a firm layer, used for bridges, heavy structures and waterfront work.

How engineers choose the foundation type

  • Soil investigation: boreholes and tests give the soil's safe bearing capacity, layers and water table.
  • Building loads: number of floors, column loads and any basement.
  • Settlement: how much the building can settle, and how evenly.
  • Water table: high groundwater affects excavation, dewatering and the choice between footings and piles.
  • Neighbouring buildings: excavation and pile driving close to existing structures need care.
  • Codes: in India, the relevant Indian Standards include IS 1904 (general foundation design), IS 1080 (shallow foundations), IS 2950 (raft foundations) and IS 2911 (pile foundations).

A simple sizing example

This simplified example shows the logic only. Real foundations must be designed by a structural engineer.

A column carries 600 kN, and the soil investigation gives a safe bearing capacity of 200 kN/m². Allowing about 10% for the footing's own weight and soil above it, the load is about 660 kN. The footing area needed is 660 ÷ 200 = 3.3 m², so a square footing of about 1.85 m × 1.85 m. In practice the engineer would round up and then design the thickness and reinforcement for bending and shear.

If the same soil had a bearing capacity of only 80 kN/m², each footing would need about 8.3 m². When footings start to overlap, a raft or piles usually make more sense.

Foundation construction steps on site

  1. Set out the grid and column positions from the drawings, and check them twice.
  2. Excavate to the depth in the drawings and check the founding soil matches the soil report.
  3. Dewater if needed, and lay PCC (plain cement concrete) levelling course.
  4. Fix reinforcement as per the bar bending schedule, with cover blocks.
  5. Fix formwork and check levels and dimensions.
  6. Get the pre-pour inspection signed off.
  7. Pour and compact the concrete, take cube samples and cure for the specified period.
  8. Backfill in compacted layers once the concrete has gained strength.

Common foundation mistakes

  • Skipping the soil investigation to save money, then over- or under-designing the foundation.
  • Not checking the founding level: building on filled soil or a different layer from the soil report.
  • Setting-out errors that put columns off grid.
  • Missing cover blocks, leaving reinforcement exposed to moisture and corrosion.
  • Pouring into water without dewatering.
  • Poor curing, especially in hot weather.
  • No record of what was found during excavation, which matters if there is a claim or a problem later.

Frequently asked questions

What are the main types of foundation?

Shallow foundations, such as isolated, combined, strip and raft foundations, and deep foundations, such as pile and caisson foundations.

Which foundation is best for a house?

Most houses on firm soil use isolated footings under columns, or strip footings under load-bearing walls. Weak soil or a high water table may need a raft or piles. A soil test and a structural engineer should decide.

What is the difference between a raft and a pile foundation?

A raft is a single slab that spreads the load over the whole area near the surface. Piles carry the load down through weak soil to a firmer layer deeper in the ground.

How deep should a house foundation be?

It depends on the soil and loads, and is set by the structural engineer from the soil investigation. Foundations must go below topsoil, loose fill and any layer affected by seasonal moisture changes.

Why is a soil test needed before foundation design?

It tells the engineer the soil's bearing capacity, the soil layers and the water table, which decide the foundation type, size and depth.

Get the ground right first

The foundation type comes from the soil, the loads and the site, not from habit. Invest in a soil investigation, follow the structural design, check the founding level on site and record what you find. A few extra days at the bottom of the building can save years of trouble above it.
