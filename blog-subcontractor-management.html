Contractor and Subcontractor Management: A Complete Guide

How to choose, contract, coordinate, pay and close out contractors and subcontractors, with a work order checklist, a meeting agenda and the records that prevent disputes.

Brikito Editorial11 Nov 20268 min readContractors

The short answer: Contractor and subcontractor management is how a developer or main contractor selects, contracts, coordinates, pays and closes out the companies that build each part of a project. It rests on five things: prequalification, a clear work order or subcontract, coordination through a single plan and meeting rhythm, measurement-based payment with retention, and recorded performance. Most subcontractor problems come from unclear scope, poor coordination between trades and payment disputes, not from the trades themselves.

The plumbing subcontractor says the bathrooms on L6 weren't ready because the masons were late. The masons say they waited two days for the electrician to finish conduits. The electrician says nobody told him the layout had changed. Meanwhile, all three have submitted bills, and the project manager has no record that settles who caused what.

On most building projects, more than half the work on site is done by subcontractors. Managing them well is the core of managing the project. This guide covers the full cycle, from choosing contractors to closing out their packages.

Contractor vs subcontractor: who is who?

RoleContract withTypical scope
Main (general) contractorDeveloper / ownerWhole project or a major package; manages subcontractors
SubcontractorMain contractor (or developer directly)A trade or package: MEP, waterproofing, façade, flooring, painting
Labour contractorMain contractor or developerSupplies workers; materials provided by others
Nominated subcontractorMain contractor, chosen by the ownerSpecialist package the owner selects; main contractor coordinates
SupplierContractor or developerMaterials or equipment, sometimes with installation

Many Indian developers act as their own main contractor and hire trade contractors directly. The principles below apply either way.

Why subcontractor management matters

  • Schedule: one late trade holds up every trade that follows it.
  • Quality: defects often appear at interfaces between trades, such as waterproofing around plumbing penetrations.
  • Cost: unclear scope leads to variations, back-charges and disputes.
  • Safety and compliance: the principal employer can carry responsibilities for workers supplied by contractors under labour law.
  • Reputation: buyers judge the developer, not the subcontractor.

The subcontractor management lifecycle

  1. Plan packages: decide how work is split into packages and which interfaces sit between them.
  2. Prequalify: check capability, capacity, finances, safety record and references.
  3. Tender and award: issue drawings, specifications and BOQ; compare like for like.
  4. Contract: sign a work order or subcontract with clear scope, rates, schedule and terms.
  5. Onboard: brief the team on site rules, safety, communication and documentation.
  6. Coordinate: plan, sequence and track work through look-aheads and meetings.
  7. Measure and pay: measure work, verify bills, deduct retention and pay on time.
  8. Manage changes and disputes: instructions, variations, back-charges and claims.
  9. Close out: snags, as-builts, warranties, final account and retention release.
  10. Evaluate: score performance for future projects.

How to choose the right contractor: prequalification checklist

  • Experience with similar work, size and building type, with references you actually call.
  • Current workload and available crew and supervisors.
  • Financial stability: can they fund labour and materials between bills?
  • Statutory registrations and compliance with labour and tax requirements.
  • Safety record and the safety practices they will bring to site.
  • Quality: visit a site they are working on now.
  • Willingness to work with your reporting and communication system.

The lowest bid is not always the lowest cost. A price that leaves no margin often comes back as claims, slow progress or poor quality.

Coordinating multiple subcontractors on site

Coordination is where most subcontractor problems start. Three tools prevent most of them:

  • A shared look-ahead. A 2–3 week plan by area and trade, agreed with every subcontractor and updated weekly, so each trade knows when its front will be ready.
  • Area handovers. A simple sign-off when one trade finishes an area and the next takes over, with photos, so incomplete work is caught at the handover, not later.
  • A meeting rhythm. A short daily coordination huddle and a weekly progress meeting with minutes and action owners.

Most of all, every subcontractor must work from the same current drawings. See construction document control and rework isn't always a labour problem.

Running site meetings that get things done

  1. Review actions from the last meeting: done, late or blocked.
  2. Progress by area against the look-ahead.
  3. Blockers: drawings, approvals, materials, access, fronts.
  4. Safety and quality issues.
  5. Next two weeks: who needs what, from whom, by when.
  6. Record decisions and actions with owners and dates, and share the minutes the same day.

Measurement, billing and payment

Payment is the most common source of subcontractor disputes, and most of those disputes come from weak records. Keep the process simple and consistent:

  1. Measure jointly with the subcontractor, by area, in the agreed units.
  2. Verify the bill against measurements, approved variations and, for labour contracts, attendance records.
  3. Apply deductions: retention, advance recovery, material issued and agreed back-charges.
  4. Certify and pay on time. Late payment drives crews away and slows the site.
  5. Keep the running account up to date so the final account has no surprises.

For labour contractors, compare claimed man-days with your own attendance and, where available, camera headcount. Our labour management guide explains how.

Variations, back-charges and disputes

  • Variations: no extra work without a written instruction from an authorised person. Agree the cost before the work where possible.
  • Back-charges: when a subcontractor's failure causes cost to others (damage, rework, delay), notify them promptly with evidence before deducting.
  • Delays: record causes of delay daily. Contemporaneous records are what settle "who delayed whom".
  • Disputes: resolve at site level quickly, escalate by an agreed path, and keep formal options (mediation, arbitration) as a last resort.

Measuring subcontractor performance

MeasureHow to track it
SchedulePlanned vs actual by area; look-ahead commitments kept
QualityFirst-time inspection pass rate; open NCRs and snags
SafetyIncidents, near misses and safety observations
ManpowerCommitted vs actual crew size
ResponsivenessTime to close actions and respond to issues
CommercialAccuracy of bills; number of disputed items

Score each subcontractor at the end of each package and keep the scores. The best way to reduce subcontractor problems on the next project is to choose better on evidence.

Closing out a subcontract

  • All snags in the package closed and signed off.
  • As-built drawings, test certificates, manuals and warranties received.
  • Final measurement and final account agreed.
  • Retention released as per the contract, after the defect liability period where applicable.
  • Performance score recorded.

Common subcontractor management mistakes

  1. Vague scope and missing interfaces between packages.
  2. Awarding on lowest price alone.
  3. Verbal instructions that later become disputed variations.
  4. No shared look-ahead, so trades arrive before their front is ready.
  5. Bills checked against the subcontractor's numbers instead of your own measurements.
  6. Late payments that drive away good crews.
  7. Meeting decisions that are never minuted or followed up.

Subcontractor management checklist

  • Is every package's scope and interfaces written down?
  • Were subcontractors prequalified, not just priced?
  • Does every subcontractor work from the current drawing revision?
  • Is there a shared look-ahead updated weekly?
  • Are area handovers signed off with photos?
  • Are bills checked against your own measurements and attendance?
  • Are variations approved in writing before the work?
  • Is performance scored at the end of every package?

Frequently asked questions

What is subcontractor management in construction?

It is the process of selecting, contracting, coordinating, paying and closing out the companies that carry out specific trades or packages on a construction project, and recording their performance.

What is the difference between a contractor and a subcontractor?

A main contractor contracts with the owner or developer for the whole project or a major package. A subcontractor contracts with the main contractor (or sometimes the developer) for a specific trade or part of the work.

What are back-charges in construction?

Back-charges are costs recovered from a subcontractor when their failure, such as damage, rework or delay, causes cost to the main contractor or others. They should be notified promptly with evidence and handled as the subcontract allows.

What is retention money in a subcontract?

Retention is a percentage of each payment held back as security for completion and defects. It is usually released in stages, for example part at completion and the rest after the defect liability period.

How do you coordinate multiple subcontractors on site?

Use a shared 2–3 week look-ahead by area and trade, sign off area handovers between trades, hold short daily coordination huddles and weekly progress meetings with minutes, and make sure everyone works from the current drawings.

Can AI help manage subcontractors?

Yes. AI construction software can turn subcontractors' messages into tracked updates and requests, record attendance by contractor, draft meeting minutes and replies for approval, and keep the evidence needed to check bills and settle delays.

Clear scope, shared plan, fair records

Subcontractors do most of the building. They do it best when the scope is clear, the next front is ready when they arrive, payment is on time and fair, and every agreement is written down. Get those four right and most "subcontractor problems" disappear.
