Construction in India, UAE, Singapore and Sri Lanka: A Market Guide

The regulators, buyer-protection rules and quality schemes that shape construction in four markets, and the site problems that are the same in all of them.

Brikito Editorial2 Nov 202612 min readRegional guide / Markets

The short answer: India, the UAE, Singapore and Sri Lanka each regulate construction differently: India through state RERA authorities and the National Building Code, Dubai through project escrow rules, Singapore through BCA approvals and quality schemes, and Sri Lanka through CIDA contractor registration. On site, all four face the same core problems: coordinating multilingual crews, materials, approvals and daily records.

A developer with projects in Chennai and Dubai, or a contractor bidding in Colombo after working in Singapore, quickly learns that construction is local. The concrete behaves the same everywhere, but the approvals, buyer-protection rules, quality schemes and contractor registration change at every border.

This guide compares four markets in South Asia, the Gulf and South-East Asia: India, the UAE, Singapore and Sri Lanka. It covers the regulators and rules a developer or contractor should know about in each, what is genuinely different, what is the same on every site, and a practical routine for running projects across more than one market.

Regulations change. This guide reflects publicly available official sources at the time of writing; always confirm current requirements with the relevant authority before acting on them.

What does "construction in India, UAE, Singapore and Sri Lanka" involve?

Construction in these four markets means delivering buildings and infrastructure under four different regulatory systems, with workforces that often span several languages and nationalities, and in climates that range from monsoon to desert heat. For a developer or contractor, the practical questions are the same in each market: who approves the design, what protects the buyer's money, what quality standards apply, who is allowed to build, and how the site proves what it did.

Why the differences matter for developers and contractors

  • Cash flow rules differ. How buyer payments must be held and released shapes the funding of construction itself.
  • Approval routes differ. The documents, the authority and the timing of approvals change from one market to the next, and a missed approval stops work.
  • Who may build differs. Registration and grading of contractors can decide which firms are allowed to bid.
  • Records matter everywhere. In every market, the developer's ability to show what was built, when and to what standard decides how easily approvals, payments and handovers go.

Construction in India

India's construction sector is regulated at national, state and city level. A few frameworks shape almost every building project.

  • Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 (RERA). Residential and commercial projects where the land exceeds 500 m² or the project has more than eight apartments must be registered with the state's real estate regulatory authority before they are marketed. Promoters must deposit 70% of the amounts collected from buyers in a separate account for that project, and update project status, including bookings, on the RERA website every quarter (Bihar RERA FAQ). Each state runs its own authority and rules under the Act.
  • National Building Code of India 2016. Published by the Bureau of Indian Standards, the NBC sets out building regulations covering planning, structural design, fire and life safety, construction practice and services (BIS: National Building Code).
  • Indian Standards for materials and workmanship. Codes such as IS 456 for reinforced concrete and IS 4082 for storage of materials govern day-to-day site practice, from curing times to how cement is stacked.
  • Local approvals. Building plan approval, occupancy certificates and other permits come from the city or development authority, so requirements vary from city to city.

On site, Indian projects typically run with crews from several states, which means several first languages on the same site. Updates arrive as voice notes in Tamil, Hindi, Telugu or Kannada as often as typed English, and the daily report has to be built from all of them. Our guide to the daily progress report covers how to keep that record accurate.

Construction in the UAE

The UAE is a federation, and much of the construction regulation is set at emirate level, so a project in Dubai follows different authorities from one in Abu Dhabi or Sharjah. Dubai's rules on off-plan sales are among the best known.

  • Escrow accounts for off-plan projects. Under Dubai's Law No. 8 of 2007 concerning escrow accounts for real estate development, a developer selling units off-plan must be registered in the Register of Real Estate Developers, needs written approval from the Land Department to advertise off-plan sales, and must open a separate escrow account for each project with an accredited escrow agent. The account is dedicated to the construction of that project (Dubai Law No. 8 of 2007).
  • Retention after completion. The same law provides that the escrow agent retains 5% of the account after the completion certificate is issued, to be released one year after the units are registered in the buyers' names.
  • Building permits by location. Depending on where the plot is, the permitting authority may be the municipality or a free-zone or master-community authority, each with its own submission process.

Sites in the UAE commonly bring together engineers and crews from several countries. Clear, structured records that do not depend on everyone reading the same language become especially valuable. Extreme summer heat also shapes the schedule: concreting, curing and working hours need to be planned around it. See our construction scheduling guide for building those constraints into the plan.

Construction in Singapore

Singapore has one of the most structured approval and quality regimes in the region, led by the Building and Construction Authority (BCA).

  • Planning permission from URA. The Urban Redevelopment Authority is the competent authority for planning permission.
  • Structural plan approval and permit from BCA. Under the Building Control Regulations, structural plans must be approved, submitted by a registered civil or structural engineer acting as the qualified person (QP), and a permit to carry out structural works must be obtained before the works start (BCA: structural plans and permit approvals).
  • CONQUAS. BCA's Construction Quality Assessment System, introduced in 1989, is a national standard for assessing the workmanship quality of new buildings, covering internal finishes, installation methods and functional tests, and external works. Where a project applies, the application must be made before construction starts (BCA: CONQUAS).

For site teams, Singapore's regime means documentation is not optional. Inspections, tests and approvals must be traceable to the element and date, which is far easier when the site's daily records are captured as the work happens rather than reconstructed at the end.

Construction in Sri Lanka

In Sri Lanka, the Construction Industry Development Authority (CIDA), which succeeded ICTAD, regulates and develops the construction industry.

  • Contractor registration. Under the Construction Industry Development Act No. 33 of 2014, contractors carrying out identified construction works must register with CIDA (D. L. & F. De Saram on the CID Act).
  • Grading. CIDA grades contractors on financial standing, technical capacity (staff, plant and machinery) and experience. Main contractors are graded from CS2 to C9, each grade with a financial limit, and registrations are valid for three years before renewal. Government contracts require CIDA registration (CIDA).
  • Development approvals. Planning and building approvals come from the relevant planning and local authorities, depending on the location of the project.

For contractors, grading links directly to the size of project they can take on, so maintaining records of past work, staff and equipment is part of staying eligible to bid.

Workforce and language on site

Across all four markets, construction depends on large site workforces, often drawn from different regions or countries. That shapes how information moves on site.

  • Several first languages on one site are normal, so written English forms are often filled in by a few people on behalf of many.
  • Voice is the natural channel. Supervisors and foremen often speak updates faster than they type them, which is why voice notes dominate site group chats.
  • Attendance and labour records matter for wages, contractor bills and compliance, and are hard to verify from a paper register alone.
  • Safety briefings need to be understood by everyone they apply to, not just signed for.

The practical lesson is the same everywhere: design site reporting around the way crews actually communicate, then structure the information afterwards, instead of expecting everyone to fill in the same form in the same language.

Climate and working conditions

Climate affects the schedule and the quality of work in every one of these markets, in different ways.

  • India and Sri Lanka: monsoon seasons affect excavation, concreting, external works and material storage. Plan below-ground work and waterproofing around them, and protect cement from moisture.
  • UAE: extreme summer heat affects concreting, curing and the hours crews can safely work outdoors. Plan pours and heavy work for cooler hours.
  • Singapore: frequent heavy rain can interrupt outdoor work throughout the year, so look-ahead plans need wet-weather alternatives.

In hot, dry conditions, curing needs particular care. IS 456 calls for longer curing periods in hot, dry weather than its minimums of 7 days for ordinary Portland cement and 10 days for blended cements. Our building construction process guide covers the floor cycle in more detail.

Questions to ask before starting a project in a new market

  1. Which authority approves the design, the structure and the building permit at this location, and how long does each approval usually take?
  2. What rules govern buyer payments, escrow or project accounts, and how do they affect construction funding?
  3. Does the contractor need registration or grading, and at what level for this contract value?
  4. Which design codes and material standards apply, and are local materials certified to them?
  5. What quality assessment or inspection regime will the project go through?
  6. What documents will be needed for completion, occupancy and handover?
  7. How will crews communicate on site, and in which languages?
  8. How will the head office see progress, cost and hindrances from this site in the same format as the others?

The biggest site problems that are the same in all four markets

Regulation differs, but the problems site teams describe are remarkably similar across all four markets:

  • Information scattered across chats and calls. Decisions, quantities and approvals are said somewhere, but cannot be found when needed. See construction information management.
  • Multilingual crews. Updates arrive in different languages and formats, and are re-typed by the office.
  • Material requests that fall through. Indents are lost and shortages are found too late. See construction material management.
  • Waiting time between activities. Crews wait for drawings, inspections and materials. See where a construction project actually loses time.
  • Costs that are explained only after the fact. See construction cost control.
  • Records reconstructed for regulators and buyers, instead of being captured on the day.

How to run construction projects across markets, step by step

  1. Map the approvals for each project at the start: planning, structural, building permit, buyer-money rules and completion certificates, with the authority, documents and lead time for each.
  2. Build regulatory milestones into the schedule, so approvals are treated as activities with owners and dates, not afterthoughts.
  3. Standardise the daily record across all sites. Use the same DPR structure, activity names and hindrance categories everywhere, so the head office can compare projects.
  4. Let crews report in their own language, and make sure the record is consistent regardless of the language used.
  5. Keep one chain for materials and cost, from indent to purchase order, receipt and issue, in each market's currency.
  6. Keep the quality trail by element and date, so inspections, tests and photos can be produced for any authority or buyer.
  7. Review all projects weekly in the same format, using planned versus actual progress and open hindrances.
  8. Check the rules again before each new project, because requirements change.

Multi-market construction checklist

  • Have the approvals, authorities and lead times been mapped for this project's location?
  • Are the buyer-money rules (RERA account, escrow) reflected in the funding plan?
  • Is the contractor registered and graded where the market requires it?
  • Do all sites use the same daily report format and activity names?
  • Can crews report in the language they actually speak?
  • Is every material request a tracked record from indent to receipt?
  • Is the quality trail kept by element and date?
  • Has someone confirmed the current rules with the authority before acting on this guide?

Frequently asked questions

Which projects must register under RERA in India?

Under Section 3 of the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016, projects where the land proposed to be developed exceeds 500 m² or the number of apartments exceeds eight, including all phases, must be registered with the state RERA authority.

What is an escrow account for off-plan projects in Dubai?

Under Dubai's Law No. 8 of 2007, a developer selling off-plan must open a separate escrow account for each project with an accredited escrow agent. The money in it is dedicated to building that project, and 5% is retained after completion until one year after the units are registered to buyers.

What is CONQUAS in Singapore?

CONQUAS is the Construction Quality Assessment System run by Singapore's Building and Construction Authority. Introduced in 1989, it is a national standard for assessing the workmanship quality of new building projects.

Do contractors need to register with CIDA in Sri Lanka?

Contractors carrying out identified construction works must register with the Construction Industry Development Authority under the Construction Industry Development Act No. 33 of 2014. CIDA grades contractors, and government contracts require CIDA registration.

What site problems are common across India, UAE, Singapore and Sri Lanka?

Scattered information across chats and calls, multilingual crews, material requests that fall through, waiting time between activities, and records that must be reconstructed for regulators and buyers are common in all four markets.

How can developers manage projects in several countries?

Map each project's approvals and buyer-money rules at the start, standardise the daily site record across all projects, track materials and cost through one chain, keep the quality trail by element and date, and review all sites weekly in the same format.

Local rules, common discipline

India, the UAE, Singapore and Sri Lanka will never share one rulebook. But the discipline that makes projects succeed is the same in all four: know the approvals before you need them, protect the buyer's money the way the law requires, and capture what happens on site, every day, in a form anyone can use.
