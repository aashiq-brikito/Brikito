How to Track Labour Attendance on a Construction Site

Eight steps to track labour attendance on a construction site, the check-in methods compared, a worked example and the mistakes that let numbers drift.

Brikito Editorial24 Nov 20265 min readSite management

The short answer: To track labour attendance on a construction site, register every worker before they start and pick one check-in method. Record each worker in and out at the gate every day, by contractor and trade. Match the headcount against the plan and what you see on site, and get the day signed off the same evening. At month end, reconcile the daily totals with each contractor's bill. Mobile check-in with a photo and location stops proxy attendance and removes the paper register.

Labour is usually the second biggest cost on a construction project after materials, and attendance is where that cost is counted. Yet on many sites attendance is still a muster roll filled by a contractor's supervisor, or a number someone sends to the group at 11 am. When the bill arrives, there is nothing reliable to check it against.

This guide shows how to set up labour attendance tracking on a construction site step by step, the methods available, a worked example, and the mistakes that let numbers drift.

What you need before you start

  • A worker list: name, ID proof, contractor, trade, wage rate or contract terms, and emergency contact for each worker.
  • A manpower plan: how many workers of each trade you expect each week, from the schedule.
  • One check-in point: usually the main gate, with fixed shift times.
  • One method, agreed with every contractor before work starts.
  • A named owner: the site engineer, supervisor or timekeeper responsible for the daily record.

How to track labour attendance: 8 steps

1. Register every worker before day one

No worker starts without being on the list. Registration is also when the safety induction happens and PPE is issued. See our PPE list for construction sites.

2. Fix the check-in point and shift times

Attendance taken at the gate at 8 am is a fact. Attendance taken by walking the site at 11 am is an estimate.

3. Record in and out, not just present

Check-out times give you hours worked, which is what overtime is calculated from.

4. Split by contractor and trade

One site total can't be checked against bills. Record each contractor's masons, helpers, carpenters and other trades separately.

5. Compare against the plan every morning

If the plan says 12 masons and 9 turned up, the shortfall is visible before it becomes a delay.

6. Cross-check what you see

Once or twice a day, count crews at their work locations. Large gaps between the gate count and the site count need an answer the same day.

7. Sign off the day

The site engineer closes the daily labour report and the project manager checks it that evening. Our daily labour report format includes a free template.

8. Reconcile with bills at month end

Each contractor's bill should equal the sum of signed daily records for that month. Any difference can be traced to a specific day and crew.

A worked example

A residential project has three labour contractors and plans for 60 workers a day. In the first month, attendance is a muster roll kept by each contractor. The month-end bills total 1,640 man-days.

From the second month, workers check in at the gate on a phone with a selfie, and the site engineer signs off the daily totals. The signed records for that month total 1,488 man-days. The contractors' bills, prepared from the same signed records, total 1,488. There is nothing to negotiate.

The point isn't that contractors were dishonest in month one. It is that nobody could prove the number either way.

How long does it take to set up?

Registering workers for a typical mid-size site takes a day or two. A paper or mobile check-in routine can start the next morning. Expect a week of reminders before check-in becomes habit, and make it clear that only recorded attendance will be paid.

Common attendance mistakes

  • Letting the contractor keep the only record of their own workers.
  • Taking attendance late in the morning, after workers have spread across the site.
  • Recording present but not hours, then accepting overtime claims at month end.
  • Different methods on different sites, so numbers can't be compared.
  • Not following up on absentees, so the same shortfall repeats every day.
  • Ignoring local rules: keep worker, attendance and wage records in the form local labour laws require.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best way to track labour attendance on a construction site?

One method used at the gate every day, recorded in and out by contractor and trade, signed off the same evening. Mobile check-in with a photo and location works well across multiple sites without extra hardware.

How do you stop proxy attendance on construction sites?

Use a check-in that proves the worker was there, such as a selfie with location, a biometric scan or face recognition, and cross-check the gate count against crews counted at their work locations.

Who should keep labour attendance on site?

The main contractor or developer's site team should keep its own record, even when labour contractors keep theirs. The site engineer or timekeeper records it and the project manager checks it.

Is biometric attendance practical on construction sites?

It can work at a fixed gate with power and network, but dust, worn fingerprints and queues at shift start are common problems. Face recognition or mobile check-in avoids some of these.

How is overtime tracked for construction workers?

Record check-out times every day and calculate hours beyond the agreed shift. Approve overtime daily, not in bulk at month end.

Count at the gate, sign off by evening

Attendance tracking doesn't need to be complicated. Register everyone, check them in and out at one point, split the numbers by contractor and trade, and sign off every evening. Do that, and month-end bills become something you check, not something you negotiate.
